Summary: Measures and trains for the gap between finding a fault-revealing input (“potential kill”) and actually supplying the correct expected output for it (“effective kill”) in LLM specification-based test generation — showing that correctness-only reward improves detection while quietly narrowing the model to less-discriminative inputs.

Sources: Liang et al. - 2026 - Correct Tests Are Not Enough Measuring and Training Oracle Conversion in Specification-Based Test G.pdf (arXiv:2609.05879v1, 5 Sep 2026)

Last updated: 2026-10-18

Motivation

Specification-based test generation gives a model a natural-language requirement (no reference implementation, no example I/O) and asks it to emit a small suite of ~5 stdin/stdout test cases in one shot. This interface creates two independent ways to fail: a test’s input can be uninteresting (every candidate implementation, correct or wrong, produces the same output), or its expected output can be wrong even though the input itself would have been fault-revealing under a correct output. Prior systems (UTGen, UTRL, CURE) reward correctness and/or discrimination but don’t cleanly separate which opportunities a joint generator finds from how many it actually converts once its own reward changes the inputs it chooses. (source: Liang et al. 2026)

This is a direct companion to Liang et al.’s earlier audit paper — same TC-Bench-derived audit protocol and author group, but here the naive single-reference oracle problem is replaced by an audited multi-reference consensus (≥5 agreeing reference programs), and the question shifts from “is the pseudo-oracle trustworthy” to “given a trustworthy oracle, how much fault-detection opportunity does the model actually convert.”

Method

  • Task: natural-language spec → JSON suite of ≤5 (input, output) pairs, no execution feedback at inference time.
  • Potential kill \(O_q\): union, over reference-valid inputs in the suite, of wrong programs whose output disagrees with the audited consensus — i.e. what the suite could detect if every predicted output were correct.
  • Effective kill \(K_q\): same union, but restricted to test cases where the model’s own predicted output is also correct (\(c_q(x,\hat y)=1\)). By construction \(K_q \le O_q\).
  • Full correctness \(C\): fraction of the 5 slots whose predicted output matches the audited consensus.
  • Oracle-Conversion Efficiency (OCE) = \(\sum|E_q| / \sum|P_q|\) — the suite-union analogue of “what fraction of discovered opportunities got converted.”
  • Balanced TDD Score (BTS): per-task harmonic mean of correctness and potential kill, to penalize gaming either axis alone.
  • Additive GRPO: reward = format + count/validity + full-correctness + effective-kill + potential-kill terms, trained jointly via GRPO/LoRA on Qwen3.5-9B. Compared against NoKill (correctness-only) and FullKill-only (drops the potential-kill term) ablations at a matched 50-step budget, plus a step-75 continuation.
  • Audited data: TC-Bench Quality-v2 split, 506 train / 56 validation / 142 evaluation tasks, ≥5 reference programs and 5-19 wrong programs per task.
  • Fixed-input source-oracle crossover (RQ3, Table 5): freezes the inputs chosen by one policy and asks all three policies to fill in outputs, isolating whether a kill-oriented policy has a worse oracle or is just choosing harder inputs.

(source: Liang et al. 2026)

Findings

  • RQ1 (joint improvement): at step 75, Additive improves full correctness from Base’s 28.59% to 42.54% (+13.94pp), input kill 24.06%→25.27% (+1.22pp), effective kill 12.23%→14.15% (+1.92pp), OCE 49.70%→56.51%. All three seeds improve jointly, though one seed’s input-kill gain is only +0.02pp — a real but modest, not dramatic, joint improvement.
  • RQ2 (the central trade-off): NoKill (correctness-only reward) reaches the highest full correctness (47.04%) and full kill (14.36%) of any 50-step arm — but its input kill (21.60%) falls below Base (24.06%) in every seed. Optimizing correctness alone narrows the model toward easier, less fault-discriminating inputs. Additive-50 is the only arm improving all four central metrics (C, O, K, BTS) over Base simultaneously, though it is not the best on any single metric — no single composite score suffices; suite-level union accounting (OCE) is needed alongside it.
  • RQ3 (fixed-input crossover, Table 5): holding inputs fixed and swapping only the oracle policy from NoKill to FullKill improves output accuracy by +1.18pp (every seed) — FullKill’s output prediction is not worse. But moving from NoKill’s chosen inputs to FullKill’s chosen inputs (keeping the oracle policy fixed) costs 3.81pp of accuracy (every seed), since FullKill’s inputs carry +2.02pp higher input kill, i.e. are harder to predict outputs for. This attributes the NoKill→FullKill correctness gap chiefly to which inputs get selected (harder-to-solve, more discriminative ones), not to a general degradation in the model’s output-prediction ability on matched inputs.
  • RQ3 (bin analysis, Table 4): in the highest-potential-kill bin (\(d_i>0.5\)), oracle accuracy craters — Additive-50 gets only 11.11% (3/27 slots) correct, vs 40.00% for NoKill and 20.69% for FullKill-only on their own highest-difficulty slots. The hardest, most fault-revealing inputs are exactly where output prediction fails most.
  • RQ4 (stability): step 50→75 continuation is not uniformly beneficial — mean full kill rises only 0.49pp overall, and only because seed 103 improves (11.92%→14.68%); seeds 101 and 102 both lose full kill (14.77%→13.55%, 14.31%→14.22%), with seed 101 also losing correctness. Step 75 is reported as the selected project configuration, not evidence that more training uniformly helps.
  • Cross-model references (Table 7, non-matched) show even much larger models (Qwen3.6-27B/35B, DeepSeek-V4-Flash/Pro) retain nontrivial O−K conversion gaps (e.g. Qwen3.6-27B: O=29.27%, K=18.27%); a proxy GPT-5.6-labeled endpoint shows a much smaller gap (O=40.17%, K=38.61%, OCE=95.80%) but is explicitly flagged as an unverified third-party endpoint, not a matched baseline.

Relation to broader wiki

This paper names and operationalizes a specific failure mode inside the oracle problem: even with a trustworthy audited oracle (unlike the naive single-reference pseudo-oracles Liang et al.’s earlier paper warns against), a test generator can still fail to convert discovered fault-revealing inputs into correct expected outputs. It gives mutation-style kill-score work a sharper vocabulary — potential vs. effective kill — for a problem that’s usually reported as a single conflated “kill rate.” It’s also a cautionary data point for test-adequacy/RL-from-execution-feedback work generally: reward shaping that only rewards correctness can silently trade away discriminative power, a dynamic worth checking for in any RL-for-test-generation setup, not just this one.
