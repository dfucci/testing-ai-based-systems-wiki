Where this sits in the agent-testing lifecycle

Most of this wiki’s agent-safety material addresses execution-time behavior: AgentSpec’s runtime enforcement intercepts and constrains agent actions as they happen. Lean4Agent addresses a different point in the lifecycle entirely — before execution, by formally verifying the workflow specification itself, and after execution, by localizing which step’s behavior violated a formal assumption.

The three-layer verification stack

(source for this section: Wang et al. 2026)

  1. Structural verification — type-checks the workflow graph (nodes/edges/variables), analogous to compiler checks on a program; catches things like a step reading a variable no reachable predecessor produced.
  2. Static semantic verification — models each step as a Hoare-style pre-/post-condition pair and proves, under an explicit “LLM executes correctly given its precondition” assumption (LLMExec), that every step’s precondition is entailed by prior steps’ postconditions. This is proof-based correct-by-construction workflow design — it can catch defects (especially graph-level, cross-step consistency violations) before a single execution runs.
  3. Trajectory verification — checks whether the LLMExec assumption held on one specific execution, combining Lean-checkable predicates, external validators, and LLM-as-judge for natural-language-only predicates, to localize the failure-inducing step.

The key empirical result: this verification is predictive of downstream task performance (workflows passing Layer-2 outperform failing ones by 11.94% averaged across two benchmarks) and actionable (using Layer-3 localization to guide targeted LLM revision — “LeanEvolve” — outperforms unguided LLM self-revision by 7.00 percentage points on a paper-understanding task). (source: Wang et al. 2026)

Why this needs a dependent-type language specifically

The paper argues prior formal approaches to agent verification are each too narrow: temporal logic can’t express data-dependent properties, SMT-backed contracts struggle with higher-order reasoning, and Hoare-style tool-call contracts only cover individual calls, not whole-workflow consistency. A dependent-type language lets a single predicate system express both per-step contracts and graph-level (whole-workflow) constraints uniformly — the ablation showing graph-level predicates catch the majority of real defects (21/40 workflows flip from failing to passing when graph-level checks are removed) is the paper’s strongest evidence for why this expressiveness matters in practice. (source: Wang et al. 2026)

Relation to broader wiki

This is a formal-methods counterpart to LLM-as-judge evaluation — the paper directly compares its Layer-2 verification against an LLM-judge baseline and finds the LLM judge misses implicit information flows and graph-level constraints that the predicate system catches explicitly (source: Wang et al. 2026). It also complements Agent Evaluation and Agent Failure Modes: where those pages catalogue what goes wrong empirically, Lean4Agent offers a mechanism to prove certain classes of failure cannot occur by construction, and to localize the ones that still do.
