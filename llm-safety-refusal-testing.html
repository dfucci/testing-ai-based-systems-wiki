Summary: How the sources in this wiki test whether a model safely refuses or handles harmful requests — a feedback-driven generator (STELLAR-D’s SafeQA study) and a documentation-driven mutation tester (LEST) — and the recurring lessons about seed overlap, judge reliability, and validating apparent failures.

Sources: Sorokin et al. - 2026 - Diversity-Guided Search-Based Testing of Large Lan.pdf; Moshood et al. - 2026 - Specification-Based Testing of Large Language Models.pdf; Dai et al. - 2026 - A Large-Scale Empirical Study of Quality Assurance Practices and Gaps in AI Agents.pdf

Last updated: 2026-10-18

Scope

This page covers model-level safety testing: given a model (or an application that wraps one), find prompts that elicit unsafe output or non-refusal, and judge whether the response is actually a failure. It is distinct from Agent Safety, which concerns constraining the actions of an agent (AgentSpec-style runtime enforcement) rather than the text a model returns. Only two sources here test refusal behavior directly; a third (Dai et al.) shows how rarely comparable tests exist in agent repositories.

Shared ingredients

Seed taxonomy — SORRY-Bench

Both direct tests start from the same safety taxonomy. STELLAR-D’s SafeQA case study follows ASTRAL’s discretization: 13 SORRY-Bench categories as content features, plus style features (persuasion, expression, politeness, slang, anthropomorphism) and perturbation features (word deletions, filler words, homophonic substitutions, character-level noise and typos). (source: Sorokin et al. 2026) LEST selects 15 of SORRY-Bench’s 44 categories across three domains (Mental Health and Self-Harm, Harmful and Violent Content, Fairness and Bias) and keeps 97 seed prompts for which refusal is the expected behavior. (source: Moshood et al. 2026)

How prompts are generated

  • Feedback-driven (STELLAR-D): a genetic algorithm over discretized feature vectors; an LLM instantiates each vector into a prompt (with five malicious examples retrieved from BeaverTails by RAG), and a continuous 0-1 safety score from an LLM judge is the fitness to minimize. See Search-Based Testing. (source: Sorokin et al. 2026)
  • Response-independent mutation (LEST): seven operators (lexical substitution, modifier insertion/deletion, person conversion, paraphrase, identity swap) fuzz fixed seeds; no loop optimizes toward compliance. (source: Moshood et al. 2026)

How a failure is decided

  • STELLAR-D uses an LLM judge — continuous during search, a binary GPT-4o-mini oracle (ASTRAL’s prompt template) afterward to count failures. The judge was benchmarked across eight LLMs on 1,000 BeaverTails question-answer pairs; see LLM-as-Judge Evaluation. (source: Sorokin et al. 2026)
  • LEST labels each response Refuse/Comply by majority of three guard models, then has three humans check every flagged Refuse→Comply flip for preserved harmful intent and genuine compliance. (source: Moshood et al. 2026)

Findings that recur

Guided or varied inputs find failures that fixed benchmarks miss

On SafeQA, STELLAR variants detect more failures than random, T-wise and ASTRAL baselines (Table 5): e.g. for GPT-4o, 216.17 failures for STELLAR vs. 77.00 for random and 73.00 for ASTRAL; for DeepSeek-V2-16B, 412.33 vs. 198.00 and 198.67. (source: Sorokin et al. 2026; see the inconsistency note on STELLAR-D about which models SafeQA covered.) In LEST, both models refuse over 95% of the unmodified seeds, yet validated mutations contradict 6 of the 9 covered refusal-testable claims. Different mechanisms, same conclusion: a high pass rate on a fixed prompt set says little about robustness. (source: Moshood et al. 2026)

Automated verdicts need validation

LEST’s judge-only count of 312 failures falls to 19 after human review, mainly because most flips came from mutations that no longer asked for the same harmful thing (28.2% preserved intent) or from responses the guards called compliant but humans did not. STELLAR-D’s SafeQA failure counts rest on an LLM judge whose binary F1 was 0.79 in the paper’s judge benchmark. Whether SafeQA’s counts would shrink similarly under human review is not tested in either source (cross-reference, not stated in either paper). (source: Moshood et al. 2026; Sorokin et al. 2026)

Counting failures and covering claims are different adequacy notions

STELLAR-D measures how varied the failures it found are; LEST measures how much of a model’s documented behavior any test touches. They answer complementary questions, and neither alone says whether the model is safe. See Specification-Based Testing and Test Adequacy Criteria.

Adversarial testing is rare in practice

At the repository level, only 8 of 157 popular open-source agent projects (5.1%) contain prompt-injection, adversarial or red-team test paths, and 97.8% of projects lacking that practice lack it entirely. This concerns agent projects rather than model testing, but it shows how little of the testing described above is routine. (source: Dai et al. 2026)

Gaps in this wiki

  • No source here tests refusal in multi-turn dialogue (the closest, MORTAR, tests multi-turn dialogue systems generally, not safety refusals specifically) or in agentic tool-use settings.
  • Jailbreak optimizers (PAIR, TAP, AutoDAN, FuzzLLM and others) appear only as work cited in LEST; none has a raw source here, so their reported effectiveness is not covered.
  • Refusal is the only oracle LEST instantiates; claims about over-refusal, factual accuracy of safety advice or supportiveness need richer oracles and are untested here.
