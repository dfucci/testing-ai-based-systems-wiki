Why code coverage fails for ML

ML code is mostly library calls into a training loop; the actual decision logic lives in learned weights, not source lines. Code coverage of the training script says almost nothing about whether the model’s decision space has been exercised. Riccio et al. 2020 found adequacy criteria the second-most-studied open problem in their mapping (12/70 papers, 17%), just behind the oracle problem (13/70, 19%). Separately, a broader count of papers that use or propose any adequacy criterion (not just papers where it’s the focal open problem) is 24/70. (source: Riccio et al. 2020)

Neuron-activation-based criteria (deep learning)

  • Neuron coverage (Pei et al. 2017, DeepXplore) — fraction of neurons activated above a threshold across the test suite.
  • k-multisection neuron coverage (KMNC) and neuron boundary coverage (DeepGauge) — refine neuron coverage into activation-range bins.
  • Surprise adequacy (Kim et al. 2019) — measures how “surprising” a test input’s neuron activation pattern is relative to the training distribution (distance-based or likelihood-based).

(source: Zhang et al. 2022; Riccio et al. 2020)

Property-oriented adequacy: metamorphic relation compliance

METTLE proposes an alternative adequacy criterion that isn’t about coverage of internal structure at all: adequacy is compliance with a weighted set of user-defined metamorphic relations (MRs), summarized via violation rate (VR) and reclustering percentage (RP). This reframes “sufficient testing” as “sufficient behavioral-property satisfaction,” and doubles as a criterion for choosing between candidate clustering systems, not just testing one. (source: METTLE)

Mutation score in the LLM era

Mutation score (fraction of injected mutants killed) remains a live adequacy criterion, but two 2025-2026 papers extend it beyond classic syntax-based mutation: LLMs can generate semantically novel mutants from a program’s natural-language intent rather than its syntax, and LLM agents can consume mutation-analysis feedback directly to strengthen the assertions they generate. Two later 2026 papers extend it further — to mutants conditioned on the existing tests, and to rubrics for LLM-judged evaluation (see “Adequacy of what?” below). See Mutation Testing for LLM Systems.

Potential vs. effective kill: decomposing mutation-style scores

A single “kill rate” conflates two different things: whether a suite’s inputs could distinguish a faulty implementation, and whether the suite’s expected outputs are also correct enough to actually flag the fault. Liang et al. 2026 (oracle conversion) formalize this split as potential kill (O, input-side) vs. effective kill (K, output-side), with the gap O−K quantifying “conversion loss.” In their specification-based test-generation setting, correctness-only reward training raised effective kill while shrinking potential kill below baseline — a joint reward is needed to avoid this trade. Any adequacy metric built on mutant-killing (not just this paper’s setting) is vulnerable to the same conflation if it doesn’t separately track whether reported kills reflect correct oracle judgments.

Adequacy of what? Suites, rubrics, and documented claims

Three 2026 papers apply the same “what did the tests miss?” question to different artifacts:

  • A test suite — Kiele et al. use surviving mutants as the gap signal: showing the LLM the base tests lifts verified-fault yield from 12.2% to 87.8% (HumanEval) without changing how subtle the faults are. Note the adequacy measure here depends on the oracle: 75.4% of the mutants that survived even the extended EvalPlus tests were real oracle gaps. (source: Kiele et al. 2026)
  • A rubric for LLM-judged evaluation — Mubric treats a defect the rubric fails to penalize (score drop under 8 overall and under 2 on every item) as the rubric’s surviving mutant, a rubric-side analogue of mutation score. (source: Yang et al. 2026)
  • A model’s documented safety claims — LEST defines test adequacy as claim coverage: a claim is covered if at least one benchmark seed prompt is mapped to it. Only 17 of 29 prompt-testable claims (58.6%) were covered, and 9 of 11 refusal-testable ones. It separates “not covered” from “covered, no counterexample observed,” refusing to read the latter as verification. (source: Moshood et al. 2026)

LEST’s claim coverage and Skill Coverage both measure how much of a document a test set exercises rather than how much of the implementation; the figures (58.6% of claims vs. 39.90-43.98% of skill constraints) use different units and are not directly comparable. See Specification-Based Testing.

Gap partially filled: agent skill coverage

This wiki previously noted no agreed adequacy standard existed for LLM-based agent systems — Tangent (Pan et al. 2026) reports practitioners have no accepted adequacy standard for agents and lean on post-deployment observability instead (see LLM Agent Testing Practice). Skill Coverage (Tan et al. 2026) is a first concrete step: a binary cover/not-cover adequacy criterion for agent skill documents specifically (not the agent’s code or weights), showing existing benchmark executions cover only 39.90-43.98% of documented skill behavior. This doesn’t close the broader gap — adequacy for the agent’s decision-making itself, as opposed to its skill specifications, remains open — but it establishes that agent-adequacy criteria are tractable to define. See Agent Skill Coverage.
