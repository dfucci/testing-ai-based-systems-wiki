Summary: An agent-based framework that, given an LLM judge’s specification, implementation and sample inputs, derives scorer-specific metamorphic recipes (a perturbation plus an expected score relation) from six fixed behavioral requirements, and validates every recipe and generated case before use. Across five scorers it yields 3.7× as many validated test families as the Judge Reliability Harness (JRH), and its faithfulness suite ranks 11 judge models consistently with FaithBench (Spearman ρ = 0.852 vs 0.100 for JRH).

Problem

LLM judges assign benchmark scores, curate fine-tuning data, give preference and reward signals and inform deployment gates. Agreement with human labels on a held-out benchmark is useful evidence but does not show how a judge responds to input changes that should preserve or alter its criterion. Behavioral testing (CheckList-style minimum-functionality, invariance and directional tests; in the reference-free setting, metamorphic relations) fills that gap, but existing judge-testing systems — CheckList, FBI (22 human-designed perturbation categories, 2,400 manually validated cases), CALM (12 judge biases), JRH — draw from a fixed, human-authored inventory. (source: Ludwick & Hsiao 2026)

The authors’ argument against a fixed library: the same transformation can have different correct expected effects for different scorers. Formatting changes may be irrelevant to a faithfulness scorer but directly relevant to a professionalism scorer; adding relevant detail should lower a concision score but raise a coverage score when it supplies missing salient information, so no single “verbosity” recipe assigns one expected relation to both. (source: Ludwick & Hsiao 2026)

Approach

Six general behavioral requirements are fixed and encoded as reusable agent instructions: monotonicity, subadditivity, repeatability, robustness, dynamic range and calibration. For a scorer s the agent receives the natural-language specification ps, the implementation and output contract cs, and representative examples Ds. It (1) identifies scorer-specific capabilities, (2) reasons about how each requirement applies to each capability to produce recipes — a perturbation and an expected relation between scorer outputs — and (3) instantiates test cases from the examples. A recipe with its cases is a candidate test family. (source: Ludwick & Hsiao 2026)

Example (toxicity): the identified capability is “implicit toxicity without profanity”; the monotonicity recipe appends belittling, profanity-free language to a neutral summary, and the expected relation is saug ≥ sbase − ε, with ε absorbing stochastic noise. (source: Ludwick & Hsiao 2026)

Validity accounting

Generation alone does not show a family measures its claim, so three binary indicators are predicted by an oracle panel:

  • Ar — the recipe and expected relation agree with scorer intent;
  • Zt,r — case t actually realizes the perturbation in r;
  • It,r — the expected relation in r applies to the realized case.

A case is joint-valid when Ar·Zt,r·It,r = 1, and Pr(J) = Pr(A)·Pr(Z | A)·Pr(I | A, Z). Per aligned recipe, n = 10 cases are sampled; a validated family needs a strict majority (τ = 6) joint-valid. A case is fault-detecting when the scorer’s outputs violate the expected relation; a validated family is fault-detecting if at least one joint-valid case is. Oracle decisions are the majority of three independent GPT-5.2 predictions; unresolved oracle predictions count as invalid and unresolved scorer predictions as not fault-detecting (conservative). (source: Ludwick & Hsiao 2026)

Experimental setup

  • Scorers: faithfulness, fluency, coherence, professionalism, toxicity. Repeatability is excluded from the primary analysis because replicating identical input needs no scorer-specific text.
  • Data: 1,200 balanced examples, 300 each from AggreFact-CNN, AggreFact-XSum, TofuEval-MediaS, TofuEval-MeetB.
  • Generation: SANE uses Claude Opus 5. JRH is regenerated with the same model for both its synthetic generation and validation stages, using its released recipes, prompts and expected relations. Neither method uses human review, editing or selection.
  • Scorer sanity check against domain benchmarks (Appendix A): faithfulness AUROC 0.707 (FaithBench), toxicity AUROC 0.803 (Civil Comments), fluency Spearman 0.331 and coherence 0.553 (SummEval), professionalism Spearman 0.336 (Stanford Politeness, one component of professionalism).

Results

Recipes, families and faults

  SANE JRH
Unique aligned recipes, all 5 scorers 108 7
Candidate scorer–recipe pairs → aligned 133 → 108 35 → 25
Validated test families 77 (57.9% of candidates) 21 (60.0%)
Fault-detecting families 31 10
Scorers with a fault-detecting family all 5 faithfulness, fluency, coherence only

The average number of aligned recipes grows with each added scorer for SANE (mean 21 for one scorer to 108 for five) while JRH’s fixed library rises only from a mean of 5 to 7. The 3.7× family advantage comes from the number of applicable recipes, not a higher per-candidate yield: Pr(A) 0.812 vs 0.714, Pr(Z | A) 0.708 vs 0.860, Pr(I | A, Z) 0.987 vs 0.986, Pr(J) 0.568 vs 0.606. On coherence JRH is more efficient when its recipes apply (faults in 3 of 4 validated families vs 4 of 11 for SANE). With τ varied from 5 to 10, SANE keeps more validated and fault-detecting families at every threshold (at τ = 10: 22 vs 14 validated, 8 vs 7 fault-detecting), though the gap narrows. (source: Ludwick & Hsiao 2026)

Human-aligned ranking of judge models

On faithfulness, the pass rate of each of 11 judge models on joint-valid cases is compared with its FaithBench AUROC (777 examples). Opus 5 (generator) and GPT-5.2 (oracle) are excluded from the panel. SANE: Spearman ρ = 0.852 (exact permutation p = 0.0015), Kendall τb = 0.734; JRH: ρ = 0.100 (p = 0.771), τb = 0.081. SANE’s pass rates span a range 2.87× wider than JRH’s, which gives similarly high pass rates to judges of very different faithfulness quality. The analysis compares model rankings, not case-by-case predictions against human labels. (source: Ludwick & Hsiao 2026)

Limitations

Stated by the authors: both harnesses use the same generator, so whether absolute generation and realization rates transfer to weaker agent models is open; the GPT-5.2 oracle panel has no human annotation, so oracle error — which would affect scorer-deficiency counts — is not estimated independently; judge-ranking alignment is shown only for faithfulness, the one criterion both harnesses support and an independent human benchmark exists for. (source: Ludwick & Hsiao 2026)

The ranking comparison used downsampled faithfulness test sets of 165 SANE and 55 JRH joint-valid cases. A “fault” is a relation violation on a case that the LLM oracle panel judged valid. The paper claims no novelty in the behavioral requirements or relation types, only in automating the scorer-specific derivation and in the validity accounting. (source: Ludwick & Hsiao 2026)

Relation to the rest of the wiki

SANE treats the judge as the system under test. Mubric instead mutates responses to test whether a rubric penalizes defects, and LEST human-validates judge-flagged failures (see LLM-as-Judge Evaluation). Validity gating of generated metamorphic tests also appears in LGMT (proof-derived relations) and Explanation-Guided MT (semantic-verification gate); see Metamorphic Testing. In related work SANE cites Cho, Ruberto & Terragni (2025) as collecting 191 relations from prior NLP research and executing 38 of them across four LLMs. (source: Ludwick & Hsiao 2026)
